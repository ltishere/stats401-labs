Lab 9: Geospatial Visualization

1. Dataset Description

This lab uses 2025 nominal GDP (current prices, billions of U.S. dollars) for the 50 largest economies, based on the IMF World Economic Outlook (October 2025). Values range from $300 billion (Kazakhstan, rank 50) to $30.6 trillion (United States, rank 1). Some figures are IMF staff estimates and may be revised later.

Country boundaries come from a world GeoJSON file (D3 Graph Gallery) and are joined to the GDP table by ISO-3 code. Countries outside the top 50 are shown in gray as "no data"; they are not treated as zero GDP. Hong Kong SAR and Singapore are too small to have polygons in this boundary file, so they appear only in the cartogram, where they are placed by longitude and latitude.

4. Design and Comparison

The choropleth encodes GDP with color on a logarithmic scale. Because the top economy is about 100 times larger than the 50th, a linear scale would make nearly every country except the United States and China look the same pale blue; the log scale separates the middle tier, at the cost of compressing ratios. In the choropleth, the most prominent countries are the United States and China, but large territories such as Russia, Canada, Brazil, and Australia also dominate visually simply because of their land area. The map preserves borders, shapes, and adjacency, so it best supports locating countries and seeing regional patterns, such as concentrations of large economies in East Asia and Western Europe and the near absence of Africa from the top 50.

The cartogram encodes GDP with circle area and uses a single color so area is the only encoding. Russia, Canada, and Australia shrink sharply, while compact economies such as Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom, and South Korea grow. Shapes, borders, and exact positions are lost, so the cartogram best supports comparing magnitudes: the United States is about 1.6 times China and seven times Japan. Moving from color to area changes perception of global economic size: color shows rank and region, while area makes the concentration of output in a few economies obvious. Linked highlighting helps reconnect distorted circles to their geography. Neither map is better overall; the right choice depends on whether the task is spatial or quantitative.
