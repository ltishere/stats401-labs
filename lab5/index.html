Fifty stations, two ways of looking at them

The same synthetic urban transit network drawn twice — once as a force-directed node-link diagram, once as an adjacency matrix — to see which questions each layout answers well.

1 — Dataset

A synthetic transit system for a medium-sized city

Loading the station and route tables…

Each station carries a district, a daily passenger volume, and a functional role — local, transfer, or terminal. Each route records how long the direct journey takes and which service runs it. The network is undirected: a route between two stations means passengers can travel directly between them in either direction without changing service.

The data is synthetic and built for visualization practice. Layout position carries no geography — two stations sitting close together in the diagram are connected, not neighbours on a map.

District is the variable a reader scans for first, so it gets colour, the strongest categorical channel; five hues from the Okabe–Ito set stay separable under common colour-vision deficiencies. Passenger volume is a magnitude, so it maps to node area rather than radius, keeping perceived size proportional to ridership. Station type has only three values and is a secondary label, so shape carries it: circle for local, square for transfer, triangle for terminal.

Travel time needed the most thought. Copying the tutorial and reading it as edge "strength" would mislead — a long journey is a cost, not a close relationship. So travel time drives the simulation's ideal edge length: slow connections settle far apart, fast ones pull their stations together, which matches how a rider experiences the network. Stroke width repeats the same value so the slowest links stay findable in a dense region. Service type uses colour plus a dash pattern, redundantly, because thin strokes lose hue quickly.

Interaction covers what a static layout cannot. Dragging lets a reader pull a tangled cluster apart and pin it. Hovering dims everything except a station and its direct neighbours, which is the only reliable way to trace adjacency once edges cross. Tooltips carry the exact figures, so no encoding has to be read precisely off the page.

A matrix with an arbitrary row order shows only that connections exist. Sorting is what turns it into an argument, so the default order groups stations by district, then by station type, then by descending volume, and draws a rule at each district boundary. Cells inside a block are within-district connections and cells outside it are transfers between districts, so the balance between the two becomes a matter of looking rather than counting. The dropdown re-sorts to degree or volume, which makes the top-left corner fill up with the busiest and best-connected stations.

All five attributes survive the translation. Link attributes ride on the cells themselves — colour for service, opacity for journey length — while station attributes move out to the margins, where a bar gives ridership and a small glyph gives the station's role. Keeping node attributes off the cells avoids stacking two meanings onto one square. The cost is real: the matrix is mirrored, so half of it is redundant, and it says nothing about paths or connectivity.

6 — Six network questions

What each view can and cannot answer

QuestionView usedWhat supports the answerObservation
1. Which stations appear central? partially Node-link Hovering dims everything but a station and its direct neighbours, so the surviving edges can be counted; central stations also sit in the interior of the layout rather than on a dangling branch. No station stands out as a hub. The best-connected keep only three edges when hovered, and at least eight stations tie at that count — among them Stations 38, 34, 33, 31, 29, 25, 23 and 21. They do sit in the interior rather than on a branch, but the gap between a three-edge and a two-edge station is small enough that hovering, not scanning, is what makes it visible.
2. Which districts are strongly connected? answered well Matrix District ordering with block rules turns district pairs into rectangles; the density of filled cells in an off-diagonal rectangle is the number of connections between those two districts. With the matrix grouped by district, the five diagonal blocks are almost entirely empty — only Central holds any within-district connections, and the other four districts have none. Nearly every filled cell therefore sits in an off-diagonal block, meaning almost all direct connections cross a district boundary. The densest pair is East × South, followed by Central × North and East × West, while East × North and East × Central are completely empty. Geographic district and network topology are essentially decoupled here, which the block ordering makes visible immediately but a list of routes would not.
3. Where do transfer and terminal stations sit? not easily Both Shape in the diagram shows role against layout position; the glyph column beside the matrix labels shows role against connection count once the rows are sorted by station type. Both encodings read clearly, but neither reveals a pattern. Triangles (terminal) are not confined to the edge of the layout and squares (transfer) are not concentrated where districts meet — Stations 34 and 31 are terminals yet carry three connections each, more than most transfer stations. Sorting the matrix by station type produces three blocks of similar density. In this dataset a station's functional role is independent of its topological position, and my design cannot show a structure that is not there.
4. Which stations carry the most passengers? answered well Both Node area gives a rough ranking in the diagram; re-sorting the matrix by passenger volume turns the margin bars into a directly comparable ladder. Node area gives the gist, but comparing sizes across three different shapes is unreliable — a triangle and a square of equal area do not look equally large. Re-sorting the matrix by volume fixes this: the margin bars stack into a clean descending ladder with Stations 50, 49, 48, 47, 46, 45, 44 and 43 at the top. Their rows are also nearly empty, so the busiest stations carry only zero or one direct connection each — volume and connectivity are unrelated here.
5. Where are the longest journeys? answered well Both Travel time sets both the stroke width and the simulation's ideal edge length, so slow routes are the thickest and longest lines; cell opacity in the matrix confirms the ranking. The heaviest lines in the diagram are Station 7 – Station 31, Station 18 – Station 34 and Station 29 – Station 50, and the darkest matrix cells confirm all three. Width alone is not precise, though: two other links I judged thick by eye turned out not to be among the six slowest, so the matrix and its tooltips were needed to settle the order. All of the slowest connections cross a district boundary, which follows from question 2.
6. Are service types concentrated anywhere? partially Matrix Cell colour against the district blocks shows whether one service dominates a district pair — far easier than tracking stroke colour through crossing edges. No block is dominated by a single service: Metro, Express and Shuttle appear in roughly equal numbers and are almost entirely cross-district, with the only within-district routes being the two Shuttle links inside Central. A few pairs do appear to lack a service altogether, for example no Metro cells in the Central × West block. Reading this is harder than it should be — at ten pixels the navy of Metro and the grey of Shuttle are easy to confuse, so the pattern is visible but needs hovering to confirm.
